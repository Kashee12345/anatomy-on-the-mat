The Hip in Three Standing Poses

Warrior I, Warrior II and Triangle all start from a wide stance, yet they ask very different things of the hip. By the end of this lesson you will be able to name the hip action in each pose, explain why the pelvis resists squaring in Warrior I, and give one cue for each pose that comes from anatomy rather than habit.

1 · See it

A deep socket and a very strong ligament

The hip is a ball-and-socket joint. The rounded head of the femur sits deep in the acetabulum, a cup formed where the three bones of each hip bone (ilium, ischium and pubis) fuse. Because the socket is deep, the hip is very stable, and it can still move in all three planes: flexion and extension, abduction and adduction, internal and external rotation.

Wrapped around the front of the joint is the iliofemoral ligament, a Y-shaped band that is one of the strongest ligaments in the body. It tightens as the hip extends. That is useful when you stand, since you can hang on it without muscle effort, but it means most people have only about 10 to 20 degrees of hip extension.

Keep that number in mind. It explains almost everything that happens to the back leg in Warrior I.

2 · Pelvis lab

Look down on the mat from above

Choose a pose. The top view shows which way the pelvis faces and where each thigh points; the gauges show roughly how much of each hip action the pose asks for. In Warrior I, widen the stance and watch what happens to the pelvis.

3 · Feel it

Find it in your own hips

  1. Stand in Warrior I with your heels in one line. Put your hands on your hip points (the ASIS, the bony front of the pelvis). Notice which way they face.
  2. Keep the back leg straight and try to turn both hip points to the front of the mat. Feel the pull across the front of the back hip, or the low back arching to make up the difference.
  3. Step the back foot out to the side, about the width of your hips, as if the feet were on two railroad tracks. Try again. Most people find the pelvis turns forward far more easily.
  4. Now take Warrior II. Hands on the hip points again: they face the long side of the mat. The front thigh has turned out to reach forward. That is external rotation and abduction.
4 · Cue it

Turn the anatomy into words for class

Warrior I"Step your feet wider, like railroad tracks, so the hips can face forward."Widening lets the back hip abduct and rotate a little, so it needs less pure extension against the iliofemoral ligament.
Warrior II"Let the front knee drift toward your pinky toe so it stays over the ankle."This recruits the deep external rotators and gluteus medius, which stop the knee collapsing inward under load.
Triangle"Reach long through your front fingertips first, then tilt. Your hand lands where it lands."The fold comes from the pelvis tilting over the femur. If the hamstrings stop it, reaching lower only bends the spine sideways.

Adaptations for all three: shorten the stance, use a block under the hand in Triangle, a chair under the front thigh in Warrior II, and a lifted back heel (high lunge) in Warrior I.
